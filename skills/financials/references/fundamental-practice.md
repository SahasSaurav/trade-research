# Fundamental practice

How the statements fit together, and the traps in reading them. Companion to
[`reference.md`](reference.md).

## The three statements are one picture

- The **income statement** reports performance over a period: revenue, costs, profit.
- The **balance sheet** is a snapshot at a date: assets, liabilities, equity.
- The **cash-flow statement** reconciles profit to cash — and is the hardest to dress up.

Read them together. A company can show profit while burning cash (accrual-heavy revenue), or show a
loss while generating cash (heavy non-cash charges). The cash-flow statement usually tells the truth
first.

## Quality of earnings

- **Cash conversion** — operating cash flow should broadly track net income over time. A persistent
  gap means profit is sitting in receivables or inventory, not banked.
- **One-offs** — gains, impairments, litigation and restructuring distort a single year. Separate
  them from the run-rate.
- **GAAP vs non-GAAP** — adjusted figures exclude "one-off" costs that recur. Report GAAP, and treat
  the non-GAAP gap as a red flag to explain, not a number to adopt.
- **Buybacks vs dilution** — a rising share count dilutes EPS; a falling count flatters it. Neither
  is operating improvement.

## Balance-sheet strength

Leverage is a spectrum, not a threshold:

- A utility with stable cash flows can carry debt/equity that would sink a cyclical.
- **Interest coverage** and **maturity profile** matter more than the raw ratio: cheap, long-dated
  debt is a different risk from floating-rate debt due next year.
- **Net debt vs cash** shows whether the company could survive a bad year without raising capital.

## Growth: level vs quality

- **Organic** revenue growth (volume, price, mix) is worth more than acquisition-driven growth.
- **Margin direction** — growth bought with discounting shows up as falling gross margin.
- **Cyclicality** — a peak-cycle margin is not a run-rate margin; say so when reporting it.

## Insider activity

- **Open-market buys** are the strongest signal — insiders put their own money in at the market price.
- **Sales** are weak signals by default: most are diversification, taxes or 10b5-1 plan sales. A
  _cluster_ of sales, or sales by several officers at once, is more telling.
- **Option exercises** are compensation, not conviction.
- Report the **role** (CEO/CFO/director) — a CFO buying says more than a director's routine sale.

## What to hand downstream

The decision agent needs **the numbers and their context**:

- profitability and its trend (margins),
- leverage and coverage (can it survive a shock?),
- cash generation and quality (does profit convert to cash?),
- insider activity, with routine sales labelled as routine.

Leave the buy/hold/avoid call and the fair-value estimate to the agent whose job it is.
